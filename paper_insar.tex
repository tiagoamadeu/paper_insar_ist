\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Geotechnical structures behaviour, Earth observation data, Machine learning, Pattern detection, Dam monitoring

\begin{abstract}
Understanding and predicting the behaviour of geotechnical structures is still a complex challenge. Traditional methods often fail to fully capture the complexities of these structures, which include challenges such as slope instabilities, dam behaviour, and landslides. To address these issues, significant advances have been made in Earth observation techniques, such as radar interferometry and satellite imagery, as well as in machine learning tools. These developments have provided new opportunities for monitoring and predictive modelling.
This review explores the application of Earth observation techniques, such as InSAR, for detecting ground displacement and deformation. It also examines the role of machine learning in analysing large datasets, detecting patterns and anomalies, and predicting potential geotechnical failures. Despite these advancements, challenges remain, particularly regarding the reliance on high-quality data and the integration of advanced algorithms into practical applications.
\end{abstract}

Geotechnical structures, particularly large-scale dams, are essential for global energy and environmental management. Ensuring these structures are safe is vital to prevent disasters. Traditionally, monitoring has focused on "observing the main actions and characterising the quasi-static structural response" \parencite{mataAnalysisInterpretationObserved2023}. These standard methods rely on physical inspections and instruments installed directly on the dam (in-situ instrumentation).

Since 1967, the most common way to analyze this data has been the Hydrostatic-Seasonal-Time (HST) model, which looks at how water levels and air temperature affect the dam \parencite{salazar2017}. However, as dams become larger and more complex, these traditional methods are becoming "increasingly insufficient" \parencite{hariri-ardebili2023}.

A major challenge is that many instruments are "placed in hostile environments, where humidity, temperature change, and freeze" test the limits of mechanical and electronic tools \parencite{desortis2007}. Because these sensors can fail or be hard to reach, there is a strong interest in satellite-based Earth Observation (EO). Specifically, a technique called Multi-Temporal InSAR (MT-InSAR) has revolutionized how we monitor ground and structure movements.

Unlike traditional sensors that must be physically attached to the dam, MT-InSAR uses radar signals to measure displacements from space with millimetre-level precision \parencite{xie2022, cernuto2026}. Because it works in any weather or lighting condition, this technology provides a way to monitor dams from a distance, even in remote areas. These satellite-based frameworks offer a more robust and continuous way to ensure safety, acting as a powerful complement to traditional monitoring.

\subsection{Limitações (referidas por outros autores)}

Despite these benefits, practical integration remains challenging. Machine Learning (ML) models, though often more capable than HST of reproducing non-linear effects, typically focus on specific case studies or arch dam typologies, which represent only 5\% of dams worldwide \parencite{salazarAnomalyDetectionDam2021}. Furthermore, the technical nature of radar data presents a barrier; the "practical utility of solely LOS (Line-of-Sight) deformation is limited for users unfamiliar with InSAR," often leading to confusion among dam managers accustomed to horizontal and vertical displacement \parencite{ruiz-armenteros2023}. Additionally, users must be cautious of EGMS data accuracy, as discrepancies compared to ground truth can sometimes reach 5–10 mm/yr due to atmospheric or geometric artifacts \parencite{crosetto2025}.

\subsection{Research Gap}

A critical research gap persists where satellite remote sensing, machine learning, and traditional monitoring operate as "isolated islands" with minimal synergy. The main issue is the lack of integration between these three areas: 

(i) The EGMS island provides regional displacement data, yet it remains abstract for dam managers due to the complexity of Line-of-Sight (LOS) geometry and atmospheric noise \parencite{ruiz-armenteros2023}; 

(ii) The Machine Learning island offers powerful modeling capabilities, but often lacks the "engineering judgement" and physical grounding required for high-stakes safety decisions \parencite{salazarAnomalyDetectionDam2021}; 

(iii) The Traditional Monitoring island provides the "ground truth" through high-precision \textit{in-situ} instruments like pendulums, but is limited by its inability to capture the wide-area behavior of the structure and its surrounding environment. 

This separation makes it difficult to turn complex satellite data into clear safety information that engineers can use. A major technical problem is that the standard EGMS "Ortho" data uses a 100-meter grid. This resolution is too coarse to show the small, local movements of a specific dam structure \parencite{ruiz-armenteros2023}. To overcome this, integrating data from both ascending and descending satellite orbits is essential. By combining these two perspectives, we can move beyond the 100-meter limitation and more accurately detect structural movements. This approach allows us to transform raw radar signals into the vertical and horizontal displacements that dam managers actually need. There is, therefore, an urgent need for a new framework that brings these three areas together, finally closing the communication gap between satellite experts and civil engineers.

Moreover, the Ortho products from the EGMS are not suitable for comparison with on-site measurements such as leveling over the dam's crest or berms." isto é muito importante para justificar a importância da combinação das órbitas ascendente e descendente}

\subsection{Solução}

To address this challenge, this paper proposes the SAFE-DAM (Satellite Automated Framework for Engineering of Dams). This framework integrates EGMS data with Machine Learning clustering algorithms to transform massive satellite datasets into operational safety indicators. Unlike purely theoretical studies, this work validates the SAFE-DAM methodology using a dataset from the Alqueva Dam. By cross-referencing InSAR-derived clusters with \textit{in-situ} measurements, this research demonstrates how satellite monitoring can complement traditional methods, ensuring that "engineering judgement based on experience" remains central to interpreting automated results \parencite{salazarAnomalyDetectionDam2021}.

\subsection{Displacements calculation and validation}

The methodology utilizes calibrated ascending (ASC) and descending (DESC) InSAR datasets from the European Ground Motion Service (EGMS) Level-2b product, representing ground displacements along the satellite Line-of-Sight (LOS). To deliver a physically meaningful reference frame for structural health monitoring (SHM), these LOS measurements were decomposed into vertical ($d_V$) and horizontal east--west ($d_H$) components. 

Rather than relying on single point-to-point pairing, which is highly sensitive to local phase noise, a robust \textit{Spatial Binning} approach was implemented. The study area was discretized into a regular grid with a spatial resolution of 50~m $\times$ 50~m, optimized via a multi-parameter grid search. This resolution successfully balances the spatial density of Persistent Scatterers (PS) over the dam body and adjacent slopes while preserving the localized geodynamic signal required to monitor both concrete structures and geotechnical embankments.

\subsection{Decomposition of Line-of-Sight displacements}

The data processing workflow, encompassing spatial filtering, grid aggregation, temporal synchronization, and geometric decomposition, was fully developed in Python utilizing high-performance geospatial and scientific libraries.

\paragraph{Spatial filtering and Grid Aggregation}
The initial spatial distribution of the EGMS calibrated points for both geometries was filtered to the dam and its surroundings, defined by fixed coordinate limits in the ETRS89-LAEA (EPSG:3035) projection ($1755050 \le \text{Northing} \le 1955850$; $2692250 \le \text{Easting} \le 2803250$). 

To mitigate spatial non-overlap between ascending and descending passes, all active PS points falling within each 50~m grid cell were aggregated monthly by calculating their arithmetic mean. Consequently, the representative coordinate of each grid cell represents the geometric center of gravity of its constituent scatterers, rather than a single operational radar target.

\paragraph{Temporal synchronization}
Since ascending and descending passes are acquired on distinct orbits and calendar dates, a standardized monthly time-series vector was established. Linear interpolation was applied to synchronize both geometries to the same temporal epochs, ensuring that the subsequent algebraic decomposition represents simultaneous structural states.

\paragraph{Trigonometric Decomposition via Cramer's Rule}
Let $d_{\text{ASC}}$ and $d_{\text{DESC}}$ be the temporally synchronized and spatially aggregated LOS displacements for a given grid cell. Let $\theta_A, \theta_D$ represent the mean local incidence angles, and $\alpha_A, \alpha_D$ the respective satellite track azimuth angles retrieved from the EGMS metadata. 

The projection of the true vertical ($d_V$) and horizontal east--west ($d_H$) displacements onto the radar lines-of-sight is governed by the following unit directional vectors:
\begin{align}
    u_{E,A} &= \sin\theta_A \sin(\alpha_A - 90^{\circ}) \\
    u_{Up,A} &= \cos\theta_A \\
    u_{E,D} &= \sin\theta_D \sin(\alpha_D - 90^{\circ}) \\
    u_{Up,D} &= \cos\theta_D
\end{align}

This couples the observed LOS displacements into a deterministic $2\times2$ linear system:
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
    d_{\text{ASC}} = u_{E,A} \cdot d_H + u_{Up,A} \cdot d_V \\
    d_{\text{DESC}} = u_{E,D} \cdot d_H + u_{Up,D} \cdot d_V
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

By applying Cramer's Rule, the system matrix determinant ($\det = u_{E,A} \cdot u_{Up,D} - u_{E,D} \cdot u_{Up,A}$) is evaluated. For cell geometries where $|\det| > 10^{-6}$, the explicit decomposition yields the isolated kinematic components:
\begin{equation}
    d_H = \frac{d_{\text{ASC}} \cdot u_{Up,D} - d_{\text{DESC}} \cdot u_{Up,A}}{\det}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    d_V = \frac{d_{\text{DESC}} \cdot u_{E,A} - d_{\text{ASC}} \cdot u_{E,D}}{\det}
\end{equation}

To eliminate reference-frame biases, the resulting time-series for each individual cell were normalized by subtracting the displacement value of the initial acquisition epoch ($t_0$), establishing a zero-baseline at the start of the monitoring period.

\subsection{Environmental Synergy and Parameter Optimization (Grid Search)}

To automatically determine the optimal combination of spatial resolution, temporal alignment, and clustering parameters, a comprehensive Grid Search (Sweep) framework was deployed. Candidate configurations were evaluated not only against geometric consistency but face their physical adherence to environmental forcing factors. 

For each tested scenario, the decomposed $d_V$ time-series were clustered using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance matrices and hierarchical agglomerative clustering. The resulting structural trends were cross-examined against a multi-variable environmental dataset extracted from the Copernicus ERA5-Land reanalysis model (spatial resolution $\sim$9~km) combined with local structural water level records. 

A physical coupling score ($Score_C$) was computed for the clusters based on the mean absolute Pearson correlation coefficient ($r$) across six key environmental drivers acting on the concrete wall and the reservoir slopes:
\begin{equation}
    Score_C = \frac{|r_{\text{Temp}}| + |r_{\text{Niv}}| + |r_{\text{Prec}}| + |r_{\text{P\_An}}| + |r_{\text{Hum\_S}}| + |r_{\text{Evap}}|}{6}
\end{equation}

where the evaluated forcing variables comprise: (1) Temperature ($Temp$), (2) Hydrostatic Water Level ($Niv$), (3) Monthly Precipitation ($Prec$), (4) Hydrological Year Accumulated Precipitation ($P\_An$), (5) Soil Moisture ($Hum\_S$), and (6) Evapotranspiração ($Evap$). This physical optimization step ensures that the selected model architecture prioritizes coherent geodynamic responses over mathematical noise.

\paragraph{Temporal interpolation}
Since ASC and DESC acquisitions are collected on distinct dates, a common set of monthly timestamps was defined. Linear interpolation was applied to generate displacement values for both orbits at the same epoch. This ensured temporal consistency and enabled direct comparison between ASC and DESC measurements.

\paragraph{Spatial interpolation}

Since ascending and descending PS points do not perfectly overlap in space, an Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation was implemented. A search radius of 25~m and a $k=5$ nearest neighbor limit were applied. The weighting power was set to $p=2$, following the standard inverse-square law (\hl{\mbox colocar referência}). This spatial resampling is a prerequisite for the LOS decomposition into vertical ($d_V$) and horizontal ($d_H$) components, ensuring that the combined measurements represent the same physical location.

The ascending geometry was chosen as the spatial reference for the line-of-sight (LOS) decomposition, with the descending measurements being resampled to the ascending points' locations using the IDW algorithm. This ensures a consistent point-based time-series before spatial aggregation into grid cells.

% \hl{\mbox Para a interpolação dos dados, considerou-se um critério de proximidade limitado a um raio de 150m e um máximo de 5 vizinhos, onde a influência de cada ponto é inversamente proporcional ao quadrado da sua distância ao centro.}

\paragraph{Computation of geometric parameters ($\beta$ and $\gamma$)}

Two auxiliary geometric parameters are defined for each interpolated ascending (ASC) point:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta$ (orbital-geometry term) — computed as:
  \begin{equation}
    \beta = \arcsin\left( \cos(I) \cdot \cos(\text{latitude}) \right)
  \end{equation}
  where $I = 98.6^{\circ}$ is the Sentinel-1 orbit inclination.  
  In this work, $\beta$ is used as a geometric correction term derived from the satellite orbital inclination and the point latitude.  
  It is not a direct measurement of terrain slope aspect (i.e., not DEM-derived).  
  It accounts for the relative orientation between the satellite orbit plane and the local observation latitude, consistent with the trigonometric formulation implemented in the processing code.

  \item $\gamma$ (cross-track tilt) — represents the local topographic tilt perpendicular to the radar line-of-sight.  
  Given the relatively flat topography of the area, $\gamma$ was set to zero ($\gamma = 0$), which simplifies the decomposition while introducing negligible error for this site. \hl{\mbox ter atenção a isto, pois pode ser uma simplificação pouco rigorosa.} 
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textit{Note:} If a DEM-based local slope/aspect correction were applied, $\beta$ would be replaced or augmented by the DEM-derived slope aspect, and $\gamma$ by the DEM-derived cross-track tilt.  
In this study, those DEM corrections were not used.

Assumptions and implications:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\beta$ interpretation: $\beta$ is defined as an orbital-geometry term computed from orbit inclination and latitude (not DEM slope aspect). This matches the code implementation and avoids confusion with terrain aspect.
  \item $\gamma = 0$: Assuming zero cross-track tilt is reasonable for the relatively flat dam area. For steep abutments, $\gamma$ should be estimated from a DEM. \hl{\mbox ter atenção a isto, eventualmente alterar este parâmetro}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Decomposition of Line-of-Sight (LOS) displacements into vertical and horizontal components}

\hl{\mbox{\parencite{ruiz-armenteros2023}} "In this project, we estimate the vertical and east-west displacements by using Detektia's decomposition software, which is based on the nearest neighbors’ decomposition. Taking the positions of one of the geometries as a reference, each point is paired with the nearest point in the other geometry. The only compliance that they have to fulfill is that the paired points are not separated more than a certain predefined distance threshold."}

\hl{\mbox{\parencite{marchamalo-sacristan2023}} "Once the offsets were estimated and the ascending/descending pairs identified (in the case of ascending and descending dataset pairs), the decomposed movement could be computed using Equations (6) and (7), plotted, and exported."}


Let $d_{\text{ASC}}$ and $d_{\text{DESC}}$ be the LOS displacements from ascending and descending passes, respectively.  
Let $\theta_A$, $\theta_D$ be the local incidence angles (angle between LOS and vertical) for ASC and DESC, and $\alpha_A$, $\alpha_D$ their respective orbit azimuth (track) angles.

\noindent The LOS measurements relate to the vertical and east–west horizontal components via:
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
d_{\text{ASC}} = -d_V \cos\theta_A + d_H \sin\theta_A \cos(\alpha_A + \beta + \gamma) \\
d_{\text{DESC}} = -d_V \cos\theta_D + d_H \sin\theta_D \cos(\alpha_D + \beta + \gamma)
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent Solving this $2\times2$ trigonometric system yields the vertical and horizontal components:
\begin{equation}
d_V = \frac{d_{\text{DESC}} \sin\theta_A \cos(\beta - \gamma) + d_{\text{ASC}} \sin\theta_D \cos(\beta + \gamma)}
{\cos\theta_A \sin\theta_D \cos(\beta - \gamma) + \cos\theta_D \sin\theta_A \cos(\beta + \gamma)}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
d_H = \frac{d_{\text{DESC}} \cos\theta_A - d_{\text{ASC}} \cos\theta_D}
{\cos\theta_A \sin\theta_D \cos(\beta - \gamma) + \cos\theta_D \sin\theta_A \cos(\beta + \gamma)}
\end{equation}

\noindent These expressions match the implementation used in the processing code (with $\gamma = 0$ for this study).

The consistency between InSAR and Ortho displacements was evaluated using some standard statistical metrics, RMSE, MAE, and Pearson’s $r$ (Equations~\eqref{eq:rmse}, \eqref{eq:mae} and~\eqref{eq:pearson_r}), measuring respectively the average error, absolute deviation, and linear correlation between datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:asc_desc_correl} and~\ref{fig:asc_desc_correl_2}).

        \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - \hat{y}_i \right)^2 }
    \label{eq:rmse}
    \end{equation}
    % which measures the average magnitude of the error in millimetres.

    \label{eq:mae}
    \end{equation}

    \item Pearson Correlation Coefficient ($r$):
    \begin{equation}
        r = \frac{\text{cov}(y_i, \hat{y}_i)}{\sigma_y \, \sigma_{\hat{y}}}
    \label{eq:pearson_r}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Clustering using DTW Distance}

The algorithm adopted was Hierarchical Clustering using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance. The hierarchical algorithm begins by treating each time-series as an individual cluster and successively merges them based on their DTW-based distance, according to a chosen linkage criterion. Unlike conventional Euclidean metrics, DTW accounts for time shifts and non-linear temporal variations, making it particularly suited for deformation signals influenced by seasonal or delayed processes. DTW measures the similarity between two sequences $X=(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ and $Y=(y_1, \dots, y_m)$ by finding the optimal alignment path $w$ that minimizes the cumulative distance:\begin{equation}\text{DTW}(X,Y) = \min_{w} \sum_{(i,j) \in w} | x_i - y_j |\label{eq:dtw}\end{equation} 

To control the elasticity of the alignment, a warping window constraint was implemented. A window of 0\% corresponds to the standard Euclidean distance (point-to-point comparison), while higher percentages allow the algorithm to match shapes that are slightly shifted in time. In this study, the warping window was tested between 0\% and 10\% of the time-series length. A 1\% threshold was selected as the optimal balance to accommodate minor seasonal phase shiftswithout forcing alignments that would compromise the physical meaning of the clusters.

The linkage method chosen was \textit{Average}. To define the optimal number of groups, the hierarchical structure of the displacement time-series was visualized through a dendrogram (Figure~\ref{fig:dendograma}). This diagram illustrates the successive merging process based on DTW distances, where the vertical axis represents the degree of dissimilarity between groups. A manual cut-off was applied to the dendrogram to define three distinct clusters. \subsubsection{Temporal Decomposition of Clustered Time-Series using STL}

To further explore the temporal dynamics of the displacements, the Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess (STL) was applied to the cluster-mean series derived from the DTW-Hierarchical clustering. STL is a statistical method for decomposing time-series into three distinct components: Trend, Seasonal, and residuals. The decomposition follows an additive model (Equation~\eqref{eq:stl}), where the observed time-series ($Y_t$) is expressed as the sum of these components:

\begin{equation}
    Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t
\label{eq:stl}
\end{equation}

where $Y_t$ represents the observed InSAR displacement at time $t$, $T_t$ is the trend component, $S_t$ is the seasonal component, and $R_t$ are the residual components. 

In the context of dam monitoring, this separation is crucial. The Seasonal component ($S_t$) captures the cyclical elastic response of the structure and surrounding terrain to annual environmental drivers, such as temperature fluctuations, periodic reservoir loading, and precipitation patterns. The Trend component ($T_t$) isolates long-term geodynamic behavior, revealing potential structural settlements or stabilization patterns that are often masked by high-amplitude seasonal oscillations. Finally, residuals ($R_t$) provide a measure of the signal-to-noise ratio, where low residual values indicate a high degree of fit for the decomposition model.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:stl}, the STL decomposition was applied to the vertical displacement ($d_V$) for each cluster. This allowed for a clear distinction between the stable, seasonally-driven behavior of the concrete dam body and the continuous settlement trends observed in the natural terrain and abutment zones.

\subsubsection{Discussion of Results}

The spatial and temporal analysis of vertical displacements ($d_V$) at the Alqueva Dam identified three distinct behavioral patterns. The methodology effectively distinguished between the structural concrete elements and the surrounding natural terrain. 

Cluster 1 (Dam body?), characterized by a strong positive correlation with ambient temperature ($r = 0.87$), represents the exposed concrete surface where deformation is primarily driven by thermoelastic expansion and contraction cycles. Furthermore, the moderate negative correlation with the reservoir level ($r = -0.42$)  characterizes the mechanical response of the arch-gravity structure to hydrostatic pressure. As the water level rises, the increasing load induces a downstream deflection; due to the dam's arch geometry and subsequent crest rotation, this motion is captured by the InSAR sensor as a slight vertical settlement ($d_V$) (\hl{\mbox embora seja verdade, tentar encontrar referência para isto.}).

% This highlights the high sensitivity of satellite-based InSAR in monitoring complex soil-structure-water interactions. Moving beyond conventional averaged analysis, the cell-by-cell correlation validated the homogeneity of the thermal response across the dam wall. The high statistical significance ($p < 0.05$) across the identified cells reinforces the reliability of EGMS data for monitoring critical infrastructure, providing a continuous spatial overview of structural behavior that complements traditional local instrumentation.

Cluster 2 (Settlement Trend?) is characterized by a continuous and linear downward trend, visible in both the observed data and the Trend component of the STL decomposition. Interestingly, it shows a near-perfect negative correlation ($r = -0.95$) with Accumulated Precipitation, which may indicate long-term consolidation processes or a delayed hydrological response in specific areas of the abutments or adjacent natural terrain.

Cluster 3 (Geodynamic Response?) represents the terrain surrounding the reservoir. While it maintains a seasonal response to temperature ($r = 0.13$), its Trend component shows a moderate decline over the years. The correlation with the Reservoir Level is negative ($r = -0.46$), suggesting that the filling of the reservoir may induce minor settlements due to hydrostatic loading on the banks.

The STL decomposition effectively isolated the signal from the noise, with residuals generally remaining below 0.4~mm. The seasonal component is consistent across all clusters, but the Trend component clearly differentiates the structural stability of the concrete dam (Cluster 1) from the more persistent movements detected in Clusters 2 and 3.


\section{Conclusion}

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating InSAR data with machine learning for infrastructure monitoring. The DTW-based algorithm successfully distinguished the cyclical thermoelastic response of the dam (Cluster 1) from localized settlement trends (Cluster 2) and natural terrain responses (Cluster 3).

The strong correlations between displacements and environmental variables validate the physical origin of the InSAR signals. Methodologically, combining STL decomposition with multivariate analysis proved fundamental in isolating millimetric movements, offering a cost-effective and spatially continuous alternative to traditional monitoring displacement systems.
